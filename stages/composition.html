Simplify: one subject, clean edges

In one sentence

A strong photo shows one clear subject and leaves out what does not help.

Before you press the shutter, name your subject in one word. Then look for anything that pulls the eye away from it.

Check the edges of the frame. A cut-off car or a bright sign at the edge steals attention. Step left, right, closer or lower until it is gone.

Picture it

Think of a sentence with one main idea. Extra words make it harder to read. Extra things in a photo work the same way.

Example

A coffee cup on a messy table looks busy. Move one step and frame the cup against a plain wall. Now the cup is the story.

On your α7 II

Use the viewfinder, not the screen, to check the edges. Look at all four corners before you press the shutter.

Try it now

Pick one object. Make 5 photos of it, and remove one distraction in each new frame.

The rule of thirds is a guide

In one sentence

Placing the subject on a third line often gives a balanced photo.

Split the frame into three parts across and three parts down. Placing your subject on one of these lines often feels natural.

It is a guide, not a law. Centered and symmetric photos can be very strong too, for example a door seen straight on.

Picture it

Think of a tic-tac-toe board over your frame. The four places where the lines cross are good spots for the subject.

Example

A sunset over the sea: put the horizon on the lower line to show more sky. If the sky is dull, put it on the upper line instead.

On your α7 II

Show a grid in the viewfinder with MENU › Custom Settings › Grid Line and pick Rule of 3rds Grid. It is Off by default.

Try it now

Turn on the grid. Shoot one subject on the left line, in the center, and on the right line. Pick your favorite.

Lines, frames and layers lead the eye

In one sentence

Lines guide the eye, frames hold it, and layers give depth.

A leading line is a road, a rail or a shadow that runs toward the subject. The eye follows it.

A natural frame is a doorway, a window or branches around the subject. It points to the subject and adds depth.

Layers mean something near, something in the middle and something far. A photo with three layers feels deep, not flat.

Picture it

Think of a path through a garden. You walk where it leads. A leading line is a path for the eye.

Example

A street with tram rails: stand between the rails and wait for a cyclist at the far end. The rails lead the eye straight to them.

On your α7 II

With the 35mm, step close to the frame, such as a doorway, so it fills the edges. Focus on the subject, not the frame.

Try it now

Find one leading line and one natural frame on your street. Shoot a photo of each with a person in it.

Space and contrast make the subject stand out

In one sentence

Empty space and contrast help the eye find the subject fast.

Negative space is the empty area around the subject. A small person on a big empty wall can feel calm or lonely.

The subject must stand out from the background. A dark coat in front of a bright wall works. A dark coat in front of a dark wall does not.

Picture it

Think of one black word on a white page. You see it at once. Put it on a busy page and it gets lost.

Example

A child in a red raincoat on a grey beach: the red pops against the grey. Leave lots of sand around them for a quiet mood.

On your α7 II

Use a small f-number, such as f/1.8 on the 85mm, to blur a busy background. Blur adds contrast in sharpness.

Try it now

Shoot one subject against a busy background, then against a plain one. Compare which one you notice first.

Distance changes perspective

In one sentence

How big near and far things look depends on where you stand.

Perspective is how big near and far things look next to each other. Only your distance to the subject changes it.

Frame a person the same size with both lenses. With the 35mm you stand about 1.2 m away. With the 85mm you step back to about 2.9 m.

Because you stand farther back, the mountain behind looks about 2.4 times bigger next to the person. Zooming or cropping only changes how much you see. The 35mm shows more of the place.

Picture it

Hold your thumb close to your eye: it covers a whole building. Hold it at arm's length and the building looks big again. Distance did that, not your eye.

Example

A portrait in front of a temple: with the 85mm from 3 m, the temple looks big and close behind the person. With the 35mm from 1 m, it looks small and far away.

On your α7 II

Swap lenses and walk until the person fills the frame the same way. Watch how the background changes, not the person.

Try it now

Frame a person from head to waist with the 35mm. Walk back and frame them the same with the 85mm. Compare the backgrounds.

Balance, patterns and look room

In one sentence

Balance the frame, use patterns, and leave space where the subject looks.

A big dark shape on one side can be balanced by a small bright one on the other. Odd numbers, such as three people, often look more natural than even ones.

Repeated shapes form a pattern, like windows or chairs. A pattern gets interesting when one thing breaks it.

Leave look room: space in front of where a person looks or moves. Without it, the photo feels cramped.

Picture it

Think of a seesaw. A big child near the middle balances a small child at the far end. Shapes in a frame balance the same way.

Example

A row of blue doors with one red door: the red one breaks the pattern and becomes the subject.

On your α7 II

Use the Rule of 3rds Grid to place the subject on the side away from where it looks.

Try it now

Find a pattern on your street. Wait for one thing to break it, such as a person or a different color.

Work the scene

In one sentence

The first frame is rarely the best, so keep shooting from new angles.

When something catches your eye, take the obvious photo first. Then move: closer, lower, higher, left and right.

Try at least 10 versions. Often the fifth or the tenth is the one you keep.

Picture it

Think of a sculptor walking around a statue. Each side shows something new.

Example

A street vendor: take the 35mm and step closer. Frame through the stall, put the vendor on the right third, and leave look room on the left.

On your α7 II

Take one frame at a time and slow down. Change one thing between frames: height, distance or angle.

Try it now

Pick one subject and make 10 different frames in 10 minutes.

Key terms

Rule of thirds
Placing key parts on lines that split the frame in three.
Leading line
A line that guides the eye to the subject.
Negative space
Empty area around the subject.
Figure-ground
How well the subject stands out from the background.
Perspective
How big near and far things look next to each other.
Look room
Space in front of where a subject looks or moves.

Practice

Common mistakes

  • Saying the 85mm "compresses" the scene.

    Your distance changes perspective. The lens only changes how much you see.

  • Zooming or cropping instead of walking.

    Walk closer or farther to change perspective.

  • Putting everything in the frame.

    Pick one subject and keep the edges clean.

  • Taking one frame and leaving.

    Work the scene: try 10 angles and distances.

  • Treating the rule of thirds as a law.

    It is a guide. Centered and symmetric photos can be strong.

Self-check

Why does the mountain look bigger with the 85mm at the same framing?

You stand farther back. Distance changes perspective.

Your subject blends into a busy background. Name two fixes.

Move to find a plain or contrasting background, or use a smaller f-number to blur it.

A person looks to the left. Where do you leave space?

On the left, in front of their face.

Which f-number gives more depth of field: f/2 or f/11?

f/11.
