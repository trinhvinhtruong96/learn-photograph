A camera collects light

In one sentence

A photo is light that your camera collects for a short time.

Light enters through the lens. It passes the aperture, a hole that can open wide or close down. Then the shutter opens for a moment and the sensor records the light.

Exposure means how much light the photo collects. Too little light gives a dark photo. Too much gives a bright, washed-out photo.

Picture it

Think of a bucket in the rain. A wide bucket fills fast. A narrow one needs more time.

Wait too long and the bucket overflows. In a photo, that overflow is a pure white area with no detail.

Example

You shoot one street three times. At 1/1000 s it is too dark, and at 1/250 s it looks right. At 1/30 s the sky turns pure white.

On your α7 II
  1. Hold the camera with your left hand under the lens. Keep your elbows in.
  2. Turn the mode dial to A (Aperture Priority).
  3. Press the shutter button halfway to focus. Press it all the way to take the photo.
  4. Save RAW files for darktable: MENU › Camera Settings › Quality › RAW.
  5. Show the histogram: MENU › Custom Settings › DISP Button › Monitor. Tick Histogram and press Enter.
  6. Do the same under DISP Button › Finder for the viewfinder.
  7. The NP-FW50 battery lasts about 270 shots with the viewfinder. Carry 2 spares.
Try it now

Set up your camera with the steps above. Then take 10 photos of things near you in A mode.

Stops double or halve the light

In one sentence

A stop is a step that doubles or halves the light.

Photographers count light in stops. One stop more means twice the light. One stop less means half the light.

Aperture, shutter speed and ISO all move in stops. So you can trade one for another and keep the same brightness.

Picture it

Think of a staircase where every step up doubles the light. Every step down halves it. You can climb with any of the three settings.

Example

Going from f/4 to f/5.6 halves the light. Going from 1/250 s to 1/125 s doubles it. Going from ISO 400 to ISO 800 doubles the brightness.

On your α7 II

The exposure compensation dial on top moves in 1/3 stops. Three clicks make one stop.

Try it now

Take one photo. Turn the exposure compensation dial three clicks up and shoot again. The second photo is twice as bright.

Aperture controls light and blur

In one sentence

The aperture is a hole in your lens. Its size sets the light and the background blur.

The aperture is a hole inside your lens. You set its size with the f-number. A small number, like f/1.8, means a big hole.

A big hole lets in more light and blurs the background. A big number, like f/8, means a small hole. You get less light, but more of the scene is sharp.

Picture it

Read the f-number as a fraction. f/2 is a half. f/8 is an eighth. A half is bigger than an eighth, so f/2 is the bigger hole.

The full stops on your lenses are f/1.4, 2, 2.8, 4, 5.6, 8, 11 and 16. Each step to the right lets in half the light.

Example

A portrait with the 85mm at f/1.8: the face is sharp and the background turns into soft shapes. A group photo with the 35mm at f/8: every face is sharp.

On your α7 II

Turn the mode dial to A (Aperture Priority). Turn the front or rear dial to change the f-number. The camera picks the shutter speed for you.

Try it now

Put a cup on a table with a busy background. Shoot it at f/1.4, f/4 and f/16 with the 35mm. Compare the backgrounds.

Shutter speed freezes or blurs motion

In one sentence

Shutter speed is how long the sensor collects light.

Shutter speed is the time the shutter stays open. A fast speed like 1/1000 s freezes motion. A slow speed like 1/8 s lets moving things blur.

Slow speeds also let in more light. Each time you double the time, you double the light.

Use these speeds as a start:

  • A person standing still: 1/125 s.
  • A person walking: 1/250 s.
  • Kids playing: 1/500 s.
  • Running or sports: 1/1000 s or faster.
  • Frozen water splashes: 1/1000 s or faster.
  • Silky water: from 1/2 s. It gets smoother at 1 to 2 s and very smooth at 5 to 10 s, on a tripod.

Your hands shake a little. A safe start is 1 divided by the focal length.

With the 35mm, use 1/40 s or faster. With the 85mm, use 1/100 s or faster.

Your camera has in-body image stabilization (IBIS). Sony rates it at 4.5 stops. In real use, plan on 2 to 3 stops for still subjects.

That means about 1/8 s with the 35mm and 1/20 s with the 85mm. IBIS cancels your shake. It does not stop a person who moves.

Picture it

Point a laser pointer at a far wall. A tiny hand shake moves the dot a lot. A longer lens works the same way, so it shows more shake.

Example

A friend walks past a café. At 1/250 s their legs are sharp. At 1/30 s their legs blur, but the café stays sharp.

On your α7 II

Turn the mode dial to S (Shutter Priority). Turn the front or rear dial to set the speed. SteadyShot is On by default.

Try it now

Find moving water or traffic. Shoot it at 1/1000 s, 1/60 s and 1/8 s. Compare the motion.

ISO brightens what you captured

In one sentence

ISO makes the photo brighter after the light is captured.

Your sensor has one sensitivity. ISO brightens what the sensor captured, like turning up the volume.

Turning up the volume also makes the hiss louder. In photos, that hiss is noise: grainy specks in dark areas.

Noise mostly comes from too little light. ISO only makes it easier to see. Still, raising ISO in the camera beats brightening a dark photo later.

Picture it

Think of a radio with a weak signal. Turning up the volume makes the music louder, and the hiss too. A stronger signal is the real cure, and in photos that means more light.

Example

A dim party, about EV 4 in the simulator: 35mm, f/1.4, 1/125 s, ISO 1600. The wide aperture and a safe shutter speed come first. ISO fills the gap.

ISO 50 is an extra setting. It holds less detail in bright and dark areas, so start at ISO 100.

On your α7 II

Set ISO with MENU › Camera Settings › ISO. ISO AUTO lets the camera pick between ISO 100 and 6400. For night work, raise the limit with ISO AUTO Maximum.

Try it now

In a dim room, shoot one scene at ISO 100, 1600 and 25600. Look at the dark areas at 100% on your computer.

Three settings work together

In one sentence

Aperture and shutter speed let light in, and ISO brightens it.

You can reach the same brightness in many ways. Open the aperture one stop and use a shutter speed one stop faster. The photo stays just as bright.

In open shade at ISO 100, these three give the same brightness:

  • f/2.8 at 1/500 s
  • f/4 at 1/250 s
  • f/5.6 at 1/125 s

What changes is the look. f/2.8 blurs the background more. 1/500 s freezes more motion.

Picture it

Back to the bucket in the rain. A wide opening for a short time collects the same water as a narrow opening for a long time.

Example

On a sunny day, try the Sunny 16 rule. Set the shutter speed to 1 divided by your ISO. Then pick the aperture from this list:

  • Snow or sand: f/22
  • Bright sun: f/16
  • Light overcast: f/11
  • Overcast: f/8
  • Heavy overcast: f/5.6
  • Open shade or sunset: f/4

At ISO 100 in bright sun, that gives f/16 at 1/125 s.

On your α7 II

In M (Manual Exposure), the front dial sets the aperture. The rear dial sets the shutter speed.

Try it now

On a sunny day, set M, ISO 100, f/16 and 1/125 s. Shoot without looking at the meter. Then check the histogram.

Modes, metering and the histogram

In one sentence

Let the camera measure the light, then check the histogram and correct it.

In A mode you pick the aperture and the camera picks the shutter speed. Many pros work this way. It is fast and keeps you in control of the look.

When you need a fixed shutter speed, use M with ISO AUTO. You set the aperture and the speed, and the camera picks the ISO.

Metering is how the camera measures light. It has three modes: Multi, Center and Spot. Start with Multi.

The meter tries to make every scene mid-grey. So snow comes out grey, and a black cat comes out grey too. Fix it with the exposure compensation dial: about +1 for snow and about −1 for the black cat.

The dial goes from −3 to +3. The menu allows ±5, but only when the dial sits at 0. In M mode, the dial only works with ISO AUTO.

The histogram is a graph of the tones in your photo. Dark tones sit on the left and bright tones on the right. There is no perfect shape.

The one thing to avoid is a tall spike at the right edge. That spike means clipping: pure white areas with no detail left. RAW cannot bring them back.

Picture it

Think of the bucket again. A spike at the right edge is a bucket that overflowed. The water that spilled is gone.

Example

You shoot a white dress in the sun. The histogram shows a spike at the right edge. Turn the dial to −1 and shoot again, and now the lace shows detail.

On your α7 II

Pick the mode with MENU › Camera Settings › Metering Mode: Multi, Center or Spot. For a live warning, set MENU › Custom Settings › Zebra to 100+. Stripes then mark the very brightest areas.

Try it now

Shoot 10 photos in A mode. After each one, read the histogram and fix the brightness with the dial.

Review every shoot in darktable

In one sentence

After every practice shoot, look at your photos full size on a computer.

The camera screen is small, and it looks different in sun and in the dark. A computer shows sharpness and brightness much better. This quick review is how you learn from each shoot.

Picture it

Think of a coach watching a replay. You see what really happened, not what you remember.

Example

You think your portrait is sharp. At full size you see soft eyes, and the settings show 1/30 s. Next time you use 1/125 s.

In darktable
  1. Copy the photos into a folder named by date, such as 2026/2026-10-25_market.
  2. Open darktable. In the import module, use add to library on that folder.
  3. Hover over a photo and hold W to see it full size.
  4. Read the settings in the image information module.
  5. Write one line per photo: setting, result, why.
Try it now

Review today's photos this way. It takes about 15 minutes.

Key terms

How much light the photo collects.
Stop
A step that doubles or halves the light.

The adjustable hole in the lens.

The aperture size as a fraction. Small number, big hole.

How long the sensor collects light.

How much the camera brightens the light it captured.
Noise
Grainy specks, mostly from too little light.

A number for how much light a scene has.

A graph of the dark to bright tones in your photo.

Pure white or black areas with no detail left.

Practice

Common mistakes

  • Thinking f/8 is a bigger hole than f/2.

    The f-number is a fraction. f/2 is a half and f/8 is an eighth.

  • Judging brightness on the camera screen.

    Screens look different in sun and in the dark. Check the histogram.

  • Using a slow shutter for people who move.

    Use 1/250 s or faster for people who walk.

  • Thinking real photographers only use M.

    A mode with the exposure dial is a fast, pro way to work.

  • Fearing high ISO.

    Too little light makes noise. A blurry photo is worse than a grainy one.

  • Shooting JPEG only.

    Shoot RAW so you can fix white balance and exposure later.

Self-check

Your photo is too dark. Which two settings let in more light?

A smaller f-number or a slower shutter speed. Raising ISO brightens too, but it adds no light.

You go from f/4 to f/5.6. How much light do you lose?

One stop: half the light.

Which is the bigger hole: f/2 or f/8?

f/2. A smaller f-number means a bigger hole.

Snow looks grey in your photo. What do you do?

Turn the exposure compensation dial to about +1.

Your histogram has a tall spike at the right edge. What does it mean?

Highlights are clipped. Those white areas have no detail, so lower the exposure.
