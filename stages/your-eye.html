Learn from the masters

In one sentence

Studying great photographers trains your eye faster than any setting.

Every master has one thing they do better than almost anyone. Look for that one thing, and try it yourself.

  • Henri Cartier-Bresson: the decisive moment and strong geometry.
  • Vivian Maier: everyday street life, and self-portraits in reflections.
  • Fan Ho: light and shadow in the streets of Hong Kong.
  • Saul Leiter: soft color and layers seen through glass and rain.
  • Steve McCurry: color and faces that tell a story.
  • Sebastião Salgado: long documentary projects in black and white.
  • Ansel Adams: planning, exposure and printing for landscapes.
  • Peter Lindbergh: honest, natural black-and-white portraits.
  • Annie Leibovitz: portraits built on an idea, with planned light.
Picture it

Think of learning to cook by tasting great food. You notice what makes a dish special, then try it in your own kitchen.

Example

You study Fan Ho for a week. Then you find a beam of light in an alley and wait for one person to walk through it.

How to practice

Pick one master per week. Look at 20 of their photos, then copy one idea with your own camera.

Try it now

Choose one master from the list. Find 10 of their photos online and write one sentence about what they do best.

Read a photo in four steps

In one sentence

Describe, analyze, interpret and judge, in that order.

Slow down when you look at a photo. Go through four steps, and do not skip to "I like it".

  1. Describe what you see.
  2. Analyze how it was made: light, lens, distance, settings.
  3. Interpret what it means.
  4. Judge if it works.
Picture it

Think of a doctor. They look first, test second, and only then decide.

Example

A street photo of a man under an umbrella:

  • Describe: a man walks in rain past a red wall.
  • Analyze: side light, 35mm, slow shutter on the rain.
  • Interpret: loneliness in a busy city.
  • Judge: the red wall makes it work.
How to practice

Write your four steps in a notebook. After a month, read them back and see how your eye has grown.

Try it now

Read one of your own photos with the four steps. Write one sentence for each step.

Ask for and give feedback

In one sentence

Specific questions get useful feedback.

"Do you like it?" gets a polite yes. Ask "Which photo stays with you, and which would you remove?" instead.

When you give feedback, name what works first. Then suggest one change, not ten.

Picture it

Think of a coach after a match. They praise one good move and fix one weak spot. That is what you remember.

Example

You show 12 photos to a friend. She says the market photo stays with her and the beach photo feels empty. You remove the beach photo.

How to practice

Show prints or a phone album, not the camera screen. Let people look without you talking.

Try it now

Ask 3 people your two questions about your best photos. Write down their answers.

Projects build your style

In one sentence

A project with limits pushes you to see in new ways.

Limits make you creative. Use one lens for one month, or shoot one theme each week.

A series is several photos on one theme. Aim for 12 strong photos on one subject.

Picture it

Think of a song with only three chords. The limit forces the writer to be inventive.

Example

For one month you use only the 85mm and shoot hands at work. By the end, you see hands everywhere and your photos get bolder.

How to practice

Make a folder for your project in darktable and give your best frames 5 stars.

Try it now

Pick a project for the next month: one lens, one theme, or one place.

Build a 12-photo portfolio

In one sentence

Your portfolio is your best 12 photos, edited alike and shown in a good order.

A portfolio shows your best work. Twelve strong photos say more than 100 average ones.

Cull hard, keep one consistent edit with a darktable style, and choose a sequence. Order matters, just like in a photo essay.

Picture it

Think of a music album. The order of the songs makes it feel like one work.

Example

You pick 12 photos: open with a wide street at dusk, then portraits and details, and end with a quiet night scene.

How to practice

Share the 12 on a phone album, a simple website or a printed book. Keep the same edit across all of them.

Try it now

Choose your 12 best photos and put them in order.

Print your work

In one sentence

Prints show what screens hide.

A print shows sharpness, color and small mistakes that a phone screen hides. It also makes your work feel real.

Start with 5 photos at 13 × 18 cm or larger. Export them at full size, as in stage 5.

Picture it

Think of reading your writing out loud. You notice mistakes you missed on screen.

Example

Your favorite portrait looks sharp on the phone. The print shows the eyes are a little soft, so you pick a sharper frame.

How to practice

Ask the print shop which color profile and size they want before you export.

Try it now

Print 5 photos and pin them on a wall for a week.

What to learn next

In one sentence

After this roadmap, pick one new skill and go deep.

Off-camera flash lets you shape light anywhere. Video uses the same camera and lenses. Film teaches patience, because every frame counts.

Keep shooting every week. Return to this roadmap whenever a basic skill feels shaky.

Picture it

Think of a language. After the basics, you pick the topics you love and learn them deeply.

Example

You love portraits, so you learn one flash next. Night portraits in the street become possible.

How to practice

Repeat the self-checks from earlier stages every few months to keep the basics fresh.

Try it now

Write down one skill you want to learn next and one first step for it.

Key terms

Critique
A careful look at what works in a photo and what does not.
Series
Several photos on one theme.
Sequence
The order photos are shown in.
Portfolio
Your best photos, chosen and ordered.
Edit, as a noun
The choice of which photos to keep.

Warm-up

Shoot one handheld night frame at f/1.4 and check it at 100%.

Practice

Common mistakes

  • Keeping too many photos in a portfolio.

    Show 12. A tight set shows your best work.

  • Mixing very different edits.

    Give the set one consistent look.

  • Asking "Do you like it?"

    Ask which photo stays with them and which they would remove.

  • Only posting online.

    Print a few. Prints teach you a lot.

Self-check

Name the four steps to read a photo.

Describe, analyze, interpret, judge.

Which question gets better feedback than "Do you like it?"

"Which photo stays with you, and which would you remove?"

Why keep only 12 photos?

A tight set shows your best work and your style.

Which shutter speed freezes walking people?
