Big light is soft light

In one sentence

A light that is big compared with the subject gives soft shadows.

Hard light comes from a small source, like the noon sun. Its shadows have crisp edges.

Soft light comes from a big source, like a cloudy sky or a large window. Its shadows fade gently.

Size is relative. A window is a big source for a face close to it, and a small one from across the room. Closer means softer.

Picture it

Think of a flashlight and a lampshade. The bare flashlight throws a sharp shadow. The big lampshade wraps light around you.

Example

A face in noon sun shows dark, sharp shadows under the nose. The same face on a cloudy day looks smooth and even.

On your α7 II

Move the person, not the camera settings. Step them closer to a window for softer light, or into the sun for harder light.

Try it now

Hold your hand near a window, then far from it. Watch the shadow edge on a sheet of paper.

Light direction shapes a face

In one sentence

Where the light comes from decides where the shadows fall.

Front light comes from behind you. It shows few shadows and looks flat but clean.

Light at 45° to the side shapes the face with soft shadows. Side light at 90° puts half the face in shadow.

Back light and rim light come from behind the person and outline them. Top light at noon puts dark shadows in the eyes, sometimes called raccoon eyes.

Picture it

Think of the moon. When the sun hits it from the side, you see a half moon. A face lit from the side works the same way.

Example

A friend by a window: facing it gives flat light. Turned 45° away, their cheek and nose gain gentle shadows.

On your α7 II

Walk around your subject instead of moving the light. Each step changes the light direction from the camera.

Try it now

Ask a friend to turn slowly next to a window. Shoot every 45°.

Golden hour and blue hour

In one sentence

Low sun gives warm, soft light, and the time just after sunset gives a deep blue sky.

Golden hour is the time when the sun is low. It runs from about 6° above the horizon to 4° below. The light is warm and soft.

Blue hour follows sunset, and comes before sunrise. The sky turns deep blue and city lights glow.

Their length depends on where you are and the season. In Vietnam, golden light lasts about 40 to 50 minutes and blue hour about 10 to 20 minutes.

At midday, look for open shade, or put the sun behind your subject.

Picture it

The word hour is loose. Treat these times as windows that open and close with the sun angle, not as 60 minutes.

Example

A street at 5:30 pm glows orange. By 6:20 pm the sky is deep blue and the shop lights shine. Both are worth shooting.

On your α7 II

Check real times on SunCalc.org before you go. Arrive early and set up before the best light starts.

Try it now

Look up tomorrow's sunset time. Plan one photo for golden hour and one for blue hour.

Read the light before you shoot

In one sentence

Look at the shadows first, then decide where to put your subject.

Hold out your hand and turn it. Watch where the bright side and the shadows fall. That tells you where the light comes from and how hard it is.

Look into a person's eyes. Small bright dots, called catchlights, make eyes look alive. They appear when the person faces a bright area.

Picture it

Your hand is a light meter you always carry. Turn it the way the face will turn.

Example

A portrait at noon: move the person to the edge of open shade, facing the bright area. The light turns soft and catchlights appear.

On your α7 II

Use the screen to check for catchlights at 100% before you move on.

Try it now

Walk around your block and check the light on your hand in 5 places. Note which one is softest.

Light has a color

In one sentence

Every light has a color, and white balance makes white look white.

Light has a color temperature, measured in kelvin (K). Low numbers are orange. High numbers are blue.

A candle is about 1850 K and a home bulb about 2700 K. The sun at the horizon is about 2000 K and golden hour about 3500 K.

Daylight is about 5500 K, overcast about 6500 to 7500 K, and shade about 7500 to 10 000 K.

White balance tells the camera what light you are in. A higher WB number on the camera makes the photo warmer.

Picture it

Warm and cool names run backwards here. The warmest-looking light has the lowest kelvin number.

Example

Your camera is set to Daylight in deep shade. The photo turns blue. Switch to Shade and the skin looks natural again.

On your α7 II

Use MENU › Camera Settings › White Balance. Pick a preset like Daylight or Shade, or set kelvin with C.Temp./Filter from 2500 to 9900 K.

Auto white balance gets fooled by strong colors and mixed lights. Shoot RAW so you can fix it later.

Try it now

At sunset, shoot one scene with Auto, Daylight and Shade white balance. Compare the colors.

Colors that work together

In one sentence

A few colors that work well together make a photo calm and strong.

Complementary colors sit opposite each other on the color wheel, like orange and blue. Together they pop.

Analogous colors sit next to each other, like yellow, orange and red. Together they feel calm.

A limited palette uses only two or three colors in the whole frame. It looks clean and planned.

Picture it

Think of the color wheel as a map. Opposite corners clash in a good way. Neighbors get along.

Example

An orange taxi on a blue-shadowed street at dusk: the two opposites make the taxi jump out.

On your α7 II

Walk around until the background color works with your subject. A step left can swap a green wall for a blue one.

Try it now

Find 5 scenes with orange against blue, or red against green.

Shape light for free

In one sentence

You can soften and bounce light with things you already have.

A white wall bounces light back into shadows. Stand a person near it and their dark side gets lighter.

A cheap 5-in-1 reflector does the same, and folds into a bag. A thin white curtain turns a hard window light into a soft one.

Picture it

Think of a mirror on a sunny day. It throws light where you point it. A reflector does that more gently.

Example

Backlit portrait at golden hour: the face is dark. A friend holds a white reflector in front, and the face lights up.

On your α7 II

With a reflector, raise the exposure less. Check the histogram to keep the bright sky from clipping.

Try it now

Put a person by a window. Hold a white sheet of paper or a towel on the dark side and watch the shadow lift.

Key terms

Hard light
Light from a small source, with crisp shadow edges.
Soft light
Light from a large source, with gentle shadow edges.
Catchlight
The light source reflected in the eyes.
Golden hour
Time when the sun is low and the light is warm.
Blue hour
Time just after sunset or before sunrise, with a deep blue sky.
Color temperature
The color of light, measured in kelvin.
White balance
The camera setting that makes white look white.

Move the light, change the color

Turn the light around the face and switch between hard and soft light. Then pick a scene light and set the camera's white balance until the white card looks white.

Practice

Common mistakes

  • Thinking noon sun is the best light.

    Low sun is softer and warmer. Noon makes hard shadows.

  • Thinking soft light means dim light.

    Soft light comes from a big source. It can be bright.

  • Thinking a higher WB number makes a cooler photo.

    A higher number makes the photo warmer.

  • Trusting AWB everywhere.

    Strong colors and mixed light fool it. Shoot RAW.

  • Planning on golden hour lasting 60 minutes.

    It depends on where you are. Check SunCalc.org.

Self-check

How do you make window light softer?

Move the person closer to the window, or hang a thin white curtain.

Your camera is on Daylight in deep shade. What color cast do you get?

Blue. Choose Shade or a higher kelvin number.

Half the face is in shadow. Where is the light?

At the side, about 90°.

Why shoot RAW in tricky light?

You can set white balance later in darktable.

Why does the background look bigger with the 85mm at the same framing?

You stand farther back.
